# Lesson 1.1 — What Is a Computer?

**Phase:** 1 — Absolute Basics
**Prerequisite:** None
**Time to complete:** 20 minutes

---

## Simple Explanation

A computer is a machine that follows instructions.

That is the entire definition. A computer does exactly what it is told to do — nothing more, nothing less.

When you click a button on a website, you are giving the computer an instruction. The computer reads that instruction, does something, and shows you a result.

Think of a computer like a very fast, very obedient worker. You give it a task. It does the task immediately, perfectly, and without complaining. But it cannot think on its own. It only does what it is programmed to do.

---

## Real-World Analogy

Imagine a vending machine.

You press button B3. The machine reads your instruction (B3), finds the item in position B3, and delivers it.

If you press B3 and the machine gives you B3, it is working correctly.
If someone reprograms the machine to give item D5 when you press B3, the machine will still obey — but now it is doing the wrong thing.

Computers are the same. They obey instructions perfectly. The danger comes from giving a computer the wrong instructions — and that is exactly what attackers do.

---

## Deeper Explanation

A computer is made of hardware and software.

Hardware is the physical parts you can touch:
- The processor (CPU) — this is the "brain" that executes instructions
- Memory (RAM) — this is short-term storage for things happening right now
- Storage (hard drive or SSD) — this is long-term storage for files
- Network card — this allows the computer to connect to other computers
- Input/output devices — keyboard, screen, mouse

Software is the instructions the computer follows:
- The operating system (Windows, Linux, macOS) — the main manager
- Applications (browser, Word, antivirus) — programs that do specific tasks

When you open a program, the CPU reads the program's instructions from storage into memory, then executes them one by one.

---

## Technical Breakdown

At the deepest level, a computer understands only two things: 0 and 1.

Everything you see on your screen — text, images, videos, games — is ultimately stored and processed as a sequence of 0s and 1s. This is called binary.

The CPU processes billions of these 0s and 1s every second. It has an instruction set — a vocabulary of operations it knows how to perform (add two numbers, read from memory, write to memory, jump to a different instruction, etc.).

Programs are written by humans in human-readable languages (like Python or C++), then translated into this binary language that the CPU understands.

---

## SOC Perspective

As a SOC Analyst, you need to understand computers because:

Every security event happens on a computer.
Every log is generated by a computer.
Every attack targets a computer (or uses one as a tool).

Understanding that a computer does exactly what it is instructed to do helps you understand attacks:

Malware is simply malicious instructions given to a computer.
A hacker's goal is to replace the computer's normal instructions with malicious ones.
Your goal as an analyst is to detect when a computer is following instructions it should not be following.

When you see a log that says a program ran, you should think:
"Who told this program to run? Was it a user? Or was it something else?"

---

## Real Examples

Example 1:
A computer in your organization runs a command that copies all files to an external server.
The computer is doing exactly what it was instructed.
But who gave that instruction? If it was not an authorized user, that is an attack.

Example 2:
A computer sends 10,000 network requests per minute to the same website.
A normal user does not do this. Something is controlling the computer and sending those requests automatically — this could be malware.

Example 3:
A computer logs in to a system at 3:00 AM when no employees are working.
Computers do not log in on their own. Something (or someone) gave that instruction.

---

## Common Mistakes Beginners Make

Mistake 1: Thinking computers are intelligent.
Reality: Computers are not intelligent. They are obedient. They do exactly what they are told.

Mistake 2: Thinking "the computer did something strange on its own."
Reality: Nothing happens on a computer without an instruction. If something strange happened, something (or someone) caused it.

Mistake 3: Thinking the threat is the computer itself.
Reality: The threat is the instruction — the software or the person behind it.

---

## Summary

A computer is a machine that follows instructions exactly.
It is made of hardware (physical parts) and software (instructions).
Everything that happens on a computer happens because something told it to.
As a SOC analyst, your job is to find out who or what gave suspicious instructions — and stop them.
